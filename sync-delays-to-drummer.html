How to sync delays and arps to a live drummer, with no click and no in-ears

Dotted-eighth delays, synth arps, tremolo, a sequenced pad: they all need a tempo. In a band that plays to a click, that's easy. Everybody follows the click and the gear follows the click too.

Most bands don't work that way. Small churches, bar bands and school groups often have no in-ear monitors, so there's no way to put a click in the drummer's ears. And plenty of drummers who could use a click would rather not, because pushing a chorus and laying back on a bridge is the point. Either way, the keys player's delays end up at one tempo while the drummer is at another.

Here are the three ways bands handle it, and what each one costs you.

1. Tap it by hand

Somebody (usually the keys player or guitarist) hits tap tempo at the top of every song. It works, kind of. The tempo is only as good as the four taps, it's frozen after that, and the drummer is not a metronome. By the second chorus the repeats are smearing against the snare.

Cost: a free hand you don't have, and drift every song.

2. Put the band on a click

Run a click (or tracks) from a laptop or phone, send MIDI clock from the same source to the keys rig, and give the drummer in-ears. Now everything lines up.

Cost: an in-ear system for at least the drummer, someone to run it, and a band that plays to a machine. For a lot of teams that's a non-starter on budget alone.

3. Make the drummer the click

Flip it around. Instead of the drummer following a tempo, the gear follows the drummer. A beat tracker listens to the kit and sends MIDI clock, a tap-tempo message on every beat, or both, to whatever needs a tempo. When the drummer pushes, the delays push. When there's a break, the last tempo holds until the drums come back.

That's what I built DrumClock to do. Put a phone by the kit (in the cage, on a stand, or fed from a kick and snare mic), let the drummer count it in on the sticks, and the tempo is set before beat one. From there it eases toward the drummer, so fills and ghost notes don't throw it.

Cost: a phone and a way to get MIDI out of it. No in-ears, no click, nobody tapping.

The short version: if your band can't or won't play to a click, don't give your gear a fixed tempo. Give it the drummer's tempo.

Setup notes by gear

Every rig needs one of two things: MIDI clock (a steady stream the device locks to) or tap tempo (a message on every beat that the device treats like you tapping its button). DrumClock sends either or both.

Sunday Keys

Sunday Keys doesn't take MIDI clock, but its tap tempo can be MIDI-learned. Turn on Tap tempo CC in DrumClock's Outputs, open Sunday Keys MIDI Learn on Tap Tempo, and send a few test taps. After that, every beat taps the tempo for you. On the same iPad, pick DrumClock as the MIDI input; from a phone in the drum cage, run it wired.

Line 6 Helix and HX

Helix follows incoming MIDI clock when it's set to receive it in the global MIDI/tempo settings. Send clock over a USB MIDI interface and 5-pin cable, and any delay set to a note value follows the drummer.

Strymon and other MIDI pedals

Strymon's MIDI-equipped pedals (and most boards with a MIDI hub) lock to MIDI clock. Feed clock into the first device and pass it through the chain. Delays set to subdivisions follow the drummer instead of the last tap.

Ableton Live and MainStage

Both can follow external MIDI clock. In Ableton, enable Sync on the incoming MIDI port; in MainStage, set the concert to take its tempo from MIDI clock input. Arps, LFOs and tempo-synced plug-ins then ride the drummer's time. A Mac on the same network can take it over Network MIDI.

Hardware synths and arps

Anything with a MIDI clock input works. Set the synth to external clock and the arp follows.

Getting MIDI off the phone

  • Wired USB MIDI: tightest. A class-compliant interface, a USB-C hub with power passthrough, and a 5-pin cable. For long runs, DIN-to-XLR adapters through a spare snake channel.
  • Same iPad: if Sunday Keys runs on the iPad that's listening, there's no cable at all.
  • Network MIDI: good over Ethernet, so-so over Wi-Fi.
  • Bluetooth MIDI: fine for rehearsal, too jittery for clock on a stage.
