Delay pedals that follow the drummer: mic the kit, Scarlett MIDI out, no more tap tempo

I've been the guitar player stomping tap tempo between parts for most of thirty-five years. Four taps on the delay pedal at the top of the song, hope the drummer counts it in at the tempo I tapped, and fix it again before the chorus. The first two bars are always a little wrong, and by the bridge the dotted eighths are smearing against the snare because the drummer pushed and the pedal didn't.

Here's the rig that got my foot off that switch. It's three pieces, one cable to the iPad, and nobody in the band has to wear in-ears or play to a click.

The idea: instead of you tapping the tempo into the pedal, a mic by the drums feeds an audio interface, the interface feeds the iPad, and DrumClock sends MIDI clock back out of the interface into your pedalboard. The pedal follows the drummer. You just play.

What you need

  1. An audio interface with a 5-pin MIDI out. I use a Focusrite Scarlett. In the current 4th generation that means the 4i4 or bigger (16i16, 18i16, 18i20). The Solo and the 2i2 have no MIDI jacks, so they can't do this job. A 3rd-gen 4i4 works too. Any class-compliant interface with a MIDI out does the same thing; the Scarlett is just the one that's on every stage already.
  2. A dynamic mic on a short boom by the kit. Nothing special. An SM57 or whatever's spare, pointed between the kick and the snare, a couple of feet off the floor. A real mic by the drums hears the kick and the snare a lot better than a phone on a music stand by the PA.
  3. An iPad on your music stand running DrumClock, plugged into the interface with a USB-C cable. The iPad goes where you can see it, because the whole point is watching the ring lock before you play.
  4. A 5-pin MIDI cable from the Scarlett's MIDI Out to the first pedal that takes clock.

Setting it up

1. The interface

Update the Scarlett's firmware on a computer first; Focusrite asks for that before it'll behave on an iPad. Plug the mic into input 1, bring the gain up until the kick is lighting the halo green on the hits and not clipping red. Then plug the USB-C into the iPad. The 4th-gen 4i4 wants more power than some iPads will give it, so if the Scarlett won't light up, use the power supply that came in the box on its DC jack, or go through a powered USB-C hub.

2. DrumClock

Open DrumClock. When the interface is plugged in, that's the iPad's microphone as far as iOS is concerned, so DrumClock hears the drums through the Scarlett with nothing to pick. Have the drummer play a bar of time and watch the ring: it should pulse on the beat and the BPM should settle. If it's chasing the hi-hat, move the mic closer to the kick.

In Outputs, turn on MIDI clock and point it at the Scarlett's MIDI port, which shows up by name once the cable is in. Leave tap tempo off unless a pedal needs it; clock is the tighter of the two for delays.

3. The pedals

Any pedal with a MIDI clock input works. On the ones most of us run:

  • Line 6 Helix, HX Stomp, HX Effects: Global Settings → MIDI/Tempo, set MIDI Clock to receive. Any delay with its time set to a note value follows.
  • Strymon Timeline, Volante, BigSky: MIDI clock sync is on by default on most of them; check the MIDI menu if the time knob isn't moving.
  • Boss DD-500, DD-200, RV-500: set MIDI Sync to Auto or MIDI in the system menu.
  • Eventide H9, TimeFactor: set the clock source to MIDI in the system settings.

Chain the MIDI Thru from the first pedal into the next one and the whole board follows. If a pedal only has tap tempo and no clock, DrumClock can send a tap message on every beat instead; see the delays-and-arps guide for that.

What it feels like on the gig

The drummer counts the song in on the sticks and the tempo is set before beat one, so the first downbeat is right. When the drummer pushes the chorus, the repeats push with him. On a drum break or a rubato bridge the last tempo holds until the drums come back, and it picks the drummer up again when he does.

Two things I lean on: presets, one per song in set order, so the clock has a starting tempo and only follows the drummer inside eight BPM of it, which means a big fill doesn't throw it; and the ring on the stand, because after thirty-five years of tapping I wanted to see it lock, not trust it.

Where it gets harder

  • Brushes and a quiet jazz kit: less transient for the mic to grab. Get the mic closer to the snare.
  • A loud PA next to the kit: the mic hears the bass rig. Point it away from the subs and tighten the gain.
  • Placement: this is most of it. Between kick and snare, close, off the floor.

Try it at rehearsal first

Honestly, start with just the iPad on the stand, no interface, no cable, and watch the ring for one rehearsal. The thing that sold me was looking down and seeing the number move with the drummer. Then add the mic and the Scarlett and run clock into the board the next week.
